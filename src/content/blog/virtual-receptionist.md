**TL;DR:** A virtual receptionist is a remote human (or AI) service that answers your phone, greets callers, takes messages, and books appointments so calls do not go to voicemail. It costs about $25 to $2,400 a month depending on whether it is AI, human, or hybrid. The catch for a field-service business: most virtual receptionists take a message, they do not triage an emergency and dispatch a technician, and that gap is where the biggest jobs leak out.

A virtual receptionist sounds like the obvious fix for a ringing phone you can never get to. And for a lot of businesses it is. But if you run an on-site service company (restoration, plumbing, HVAC, roofing), the receptionist's job is not just to answer politely. It is to triage an emergency and get a crew moving while the caller is still on the line. That is a different product than most virtual receptionists sell. ## What is a virtual receptionist and how does it work?

A virtual receptionist is a remote service, staffed by a trained human or run by AI, that answers your incoming calls under your business name, greets each caller with your script, takes messages, answers common questions, and books appointments, so your phone gets a live pickup instead of voicemail. You forward your line to the number they give you, and they handle calls all the time, only when you are busy, or after hours.

The setup is simple. You [forward your phone line to the number your provider gives you, either all the time, only when you are busy, or just after hours](https://www.receptionhq.com/what-is-a-virtual-receptionist/), and the receptionist answers on your behalf with a personalized greeting. From there they follow the instructions you gave them: relay a message, transfer the call, answer FAQs, or [book an appointment on your behalf](https://www.nextiva.com/blog/virtual-receptionist.html).

It helps to separate two terms people use interchangeably. A basic answering service mostly takes messages and patches calls. A virtual receptionist [acts more like an extension of your business, handling appointment scheduling, lead intake, and customer support](https://www.mapcommunications.com/blog/answering-service-vs-virtual-receptionist-whats-the-difference/). Both beat voicemail. Neither, in the traditional human version, was built to run an emergency dispatch.

## How much does a virtual receptionist cost?

A virtual receptionist costs roughly $25 to $2,400 a month in 2026, and the price splits hard by type: AI-only services run about $25 to $300 a month, human receptionist services run about $800 to $2,400 a month, and hybrid AI-plus-human-backup plans land in the $300 to $2,000 range. Most human plans sell bundles of minutes, so your real bill depends on call volume and call length.

Here are published 2026 numbers, with the per-minute rate hiding inside each bundle:

| Provider and plan | Monthly price | What you get | Effective rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Ruby Starter](https://www.ruby.com/pricing/) | $250 | 50 minutes | $5.00 per minute |
| [Ruby Professional](https://www.ruby.com/pricing/) | $395 | 100 minutes | $3.95 per minute |
| [Ruby Business](https://www.ruby.com/pricing/) | $720 | 200 minutes | $3.60 per minute |
| [Ruby Enterprise](https://www.ruby.com/pricing/) | $1,725 | 500 minutes | $3.45 per minute |
| [Smith.ai Starter](https://smith.ai/pricing) | $300 | 30 calls | $10.00 per call |
| [Abby Connect Essential (AI)](https://www.abby.com/pricing/) | $165 | 100 minutes | $1.65 per minute |

Two patterns matter. First, the cheapest plan carries the most expensive minutes: Ruby's $250 tier sells minutes at $5.00, the $1,725 tier at $3.45. Entry pricing is built to move you up the ladder. Second, most services charge [per-minute overage fees of $1.00 to $11.00 once your bundle runs out](https://www.getaira.io/blog/missed-business-calls-statistics), and [calls round up to the next full minute](https://www.nextiva.com/blog/answering-service-cost.html), so a 70-second call bills as two. The advertised monthly price is the floor, not the number on your invoice. I broke down that whole per-minute trap in our [virtual receptionist pricing guide](/blog/virtual-receptionist-pricing).

## Can a virtual receptionist book jobs and dispatch a technician?

Most traditional human virtual receptionists cannot dispatch a technician. They can take a message, book a slot on a calendar, and transfer a warm call, but they do not run your on-call rotation, triage an emergency by severity, or push a job into your CRM and ping a crew. For a message-based business that is fine. For an emergency trade, the gap between "message taken" and "tech dispatched" is where the money leaks.

![Restoration contractor on a job site taking a customer call on his phone next to drying equipment](/blog-images/virtual-receptionist-section.png)
*When the owner is on a job, the receptionist has to close and dispatch, not just promise a callback.*

Here is why that gap is expensive. When a homeowner's basement is filling with water, they are not leaving a message and waiting. They are calling until a human answers, and if your receptionist says "I will pass this along and someone will call you back," they hang up and dial the next company. In my years on the truck, the jobs we lost were almost never on price. They were on who picked up and moved first.

This is the exact thing an AI receptionist was built to close. It can [scope the situation and recommend an on-site assessment whenever there is uncertainty](https://restorationai.io), quote from your real price book, send a work authorization by text, write the job into JobNimbus or Xcelerate, and fire a dispatch ping to your on-call tech, all inside the same call. A message-taker ends the call with a note. A dispatcher ends it with a crew rolling. If your business lives on emergencies, that difference is the whole product. It is the same reason a text-back tool falls short, which I unpack in our [missed call text back breakdown](/blog/missed-call-text-back).

## Virtual receptionist vs AI receptionist: which is better for a service business?

For an on-site service business, an AI receptionist is usually the better fit, because it answers every line at once during a surge, dispatches a tech instead of taking a message, and costs a fraction of a human plan, while a human virtual receptionist still wins when callers need genuine empathy or complex judgment a script cannot cover. The honest answer is that a good setup uses AI for the intake-and-dispatch job and hands off the rare call that truly needs a person.

The side-by-side that matters for a field business:

| | Human virtual receptionist | AI receptionist |
|---|---|---|
| Answers during a storm surge | Queues, calls drop | Unlimited concurrent calls |
| Emergency triage and dispatch | Rarely | Yes, in the same call |
| CRM write-back | Manual, if at all | Automatic before hangup |
| After-hours coverage | Often a paid tier | 24/7 by default |
| Typical monthly cost | [$800 to $2,400](https://www.getnextphone.com/blog/virtual-receptionist-cost) | [$25 to $300](https://www.getnextphone.com/blog/virtual-receptionist-cost) |
| Best at | Empathy, judgment calls | Speed, volume, dispatch |

The cost logic gets worse for humans as you grow, because a live service has to staff more seats while software just answers more lines. Abby Connect proves it on its own pricing page: it sells the same minute bundles both ways, and [the AI plan costs about half the live plan, $165 versus $329 for 100 minutes](https://www.abby.com/pricing/). ## Why do service businesses still lose jobs with a virtual receptionist?

Service businesses lose jobs even with a virtual receptionist because a message is not a booking, and the highest-value calls, the after-hours emergencies, are exactly the ones a message-based service handles worst. The receptionist captures the lead, but the job still hinges on how fast the owner calls back from a crawl space, and by then the caller has often reached a competitor.

The numbers behind that are brutal. When callers hit voicemail or a dead end, [86% hang up without leaving a message](https://www.getaira.io/blog/missed-business-calls-statistics), and [71% immediately call another business](https://captureclient.com/blog/why-67-percent-callers-never-leave-voicemail). A virtual receptionist that only takes a message and promises a callback is one step better than voicemail, but it lives on the wrong side of that statistic: it still ends the call without booking the work.

> The buying criterion for a field business is not "does it answer the phone." Nearly everything answers the phone now. The criterion is "does it end the call with a scheduled, dispatched, CRM-logged job." In restoration, one missed $5,000 water job that reached a competitor first costs more than a year of any plan in this article. That is why I stopped grading receptionists on politeness and started grading them on cost per booked job.

That framing is the whole thesis of how we price and compare these tools, and it is why the pillar guide starts with dispatch, not message-taking. ## What should you look for in a virtual receptionist?

Look for coverage that matches when your calls actually come in, the ability to book and dispatch rather than just take a message, transparent pricing you can compare in cost per booked job, and CRM integration so nothing gets re-typed by hand. Start with the overage rate and the after-hours policy, not the headline monthly price.

The five-point checklist I give owners:

1. **Dispatch, not messages.** Ask directly: can it book the job, send a work authorization, and notify my on-call tech during the call? If the answer is "we take a detailed message," it is an answering service wearing a nicer name.
2. **Real 24/7 coverage.** Emergencies do not keep business hours. Confirm nights and weekends get the same handling as noon, not a skeleton crew, which is a gap I dug into in our [24 hour answering service guide](/blog/24-hour-answering-service).
3. **Surge capacity.** Storms and heat waves spike call volume exactly when a live queue overflows. Ask what happens when ten calls hit at once.
4. **Transparent, comparable pricing.** Get the overage rate, setup fees, per-action fees, and after-hours premiums in writing, then judge every quote in cost per booked job, not cost per month.
5. **CRM write-back.** The job data should land in your system before the caller hangs up. Manual re-entry is where details and follow-ups die.
