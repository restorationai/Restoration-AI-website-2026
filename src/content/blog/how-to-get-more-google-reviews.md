**TL;DR:** You get more Google reviews by asking every customer, by text, with a direct link, within 48 hours of finishing the job. Do not filter out unhappy customers, offer rewards, or ask for specific words, because Google bans all three. Aim for a steady flow of reviews over time, since freshness and consistency matter alongside your total count.

Most restoration companies do not have a review problem. They have an asking problem. [BrightLocal's 2026 consumer survey](https://www.brightlocal.com/learn/local-consumer-review-survey/) found that 78% of consumers were asked for a review in the past year, and 65% wrote one after being asked. (Another line in the same report says 83%, so I am using the lower number.) Customers will do it. Somebody just has to ask.

The stakes also went up. The same survey says 68% of consumers will only use a business with four or more stars, up from 55% in 2025. And 74% look for reviews written in the last three months. A pile of old reviews no longer carries you.

I built RestorationAI.io after my uncle lost an $80,000 job to a single missed call. Reviews help decide who gets that call in the first place.

A stack of review cards in the truck does not.

## How do you get more Google reviews as a contractor?

You get more Google reviews as a contractor by asking every customer, by text, with a direct review link, right after the job is finished. Make it a system tied to your job status, not a favor your techs try to remember. Consistent asking beats any clever script or one-time push.

Here is the system I would set up:

1. **Get your direct link.** Google says you can [ask customers to visit a Google link or scan a QR code](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3474122). Customers must be signed into a Google Account to post.
2. **Tie the ask to job status.** When the job closes in your system, the text goes out. Nobody has to remember anything.
3. **Ask everyone.** Happy, quiet, or annoyed. More on why below.
4. **Text first.** Focus Digital, a marketing agency, [reports](https://focus-digital.co/average-review-response-rate/) 18% to 28% response for SMS and 10% to 15% for automated email. It does not publish its sample size, so treat those numbers as a direction, not a promise.
5. **Follow up once.** One reminder a few days later is plenty.
6. **Reply to every review.** BrightLocal found [80% of consumers are likely to use a business that responds to all its reviews](https://www.brightlocal.com/learn/local-consumer-review-survey/).

Reviews also feed your paid leads. Google says [star ratings and number of reviews affect how you rank in Local Services Ads](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/7496631). Your [speed to lead](/blog/speed-to-lead) counts there too, since Google says [missed calls can hurt your responsiveness](https://support.google.com/localservices/answer/7527305).

For where reviews fit among your other channels, see our guides on [marketing for restoration companies](/blog/marketing-for-restoration-companies) and [how to get water damage leads](/blog/how-to-get-water-damage-leads).

## When is the best time to ask a customer for a review?

The best time to ask a customer for a review is within 48 hours of the real finish of the job, ideally the same day. For restoration, the real finish comes after dry-out is signed off and the equipment is gone. It does not come after the first visit.

Focus Digital's [timing table](https://focus-digital.co/average-review-response-rate/) shows the usual pattern:

| When you ask | Reported response rate |
|---|---|
| Within 1 to 4 hours | 18% to 28% |
| 24 to 48 hours | 12% to 18% |
| 3 to 7 days | 7% to 12% |
| 1 to 2 weeks | 3% to 6% |
| 2+ weeks | 1% to 3% |

I want to be straight with you about this data. I could not find an independent study on review timing for contractors. Vendor numbers disagree, too. [RelinkAI](https://relinkai-website.pages.dev/en/blog/best-times-to-request-reviews/) cites a study showing a peak at days 7 to 8, while also telling home service companies to ask at job completion. So use the table for direction, then rely on common sense about your own customers.

Restoration also has a timing problem most guides skip: a single job can have several finish lines. Here is how I would set it up:

| Job type | When to send the request |
|---|---|
| Water mitigation | The day equipment is pulled and final readings are done |
| Mold remediation | After clearance and the final walk-through |
| Fire and smoke | After cleaning is complete and the home is usable |
| Mitigation plus rebuild | When your work on that phase is signed off, once per phase at most |
| Commercial loss | To the person who hired you, after final sign-off |
| Fatality or biohazard | No automated text. Decide this rule by job type, in advance |

Do not wait for the insurance claim to settle. That can take months, and by then the relief has faded. The homeowner may also be upset with the carrier, not you.

## Is it allowed to filter or incentivize Google reviews?

No, Google does not allow businesses to filter or incentivize reviews. [Google's Maps policy](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114) bans offering payment, discounts, or free services for a review. It also bans discouraging negative reviews and selectively soliciting positive ones. Reviews that break these rules can be removed.

Filtering is usually called review gating. A tool asks, "How was your experience?" Happy customers get the Google link. Unhappy customers get a private form. Plenty of review software has offered this, so check how yours works.

Here is how Google and the FTC line up on the common tactics:

| Tactic | Google policy | FTC |
|---|---|---|
| Asking every customer for an honest review | Allowed | Allowed |
| Asking only customers you think are happy | Banned | [No specific ban in the review rule, but it "could violate the FTC Act"](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-reviews-testimonials-rule-questions-answers) |
| Discount or gift card for a review | Banned, even with no star rating required | [Can't be tied to a positive review, and should be disclosed](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/soliciting-paying-online-reviews-guide-marketers) |
| Reviews from your employees | Banned as a conflict of interest | [Don't ask, unless they disclose they work for you](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/soliciting-paying-online-reviews-guide-marketers) |

Most guides stop at gating and gift cards. But Google's policy goes further, and it catches some common contractor habits:

- **"Five reviews a week" targets for techs.** Google lists asking staff to solicit a certain number of reviews as a violation. I would treat a per-review tech bonus as the same risk.
- **"Mention Mike in your review."** Google specifically calls out staff soliciting content that identifies a staff member.
- **"Say it was a water damage job."** Google says merchants should not request that specific content be included.
- **The tablet handoff.** Google says not to require or pressure customers to review "while on the premises." That reads like it was written for a storefront, but I would apply it to a tech waiting in the living room. A friendly "you'll get a text from us" is fine.

Unhappy customers still deserve a call. You can absolutely reach out and try to fix things. Just send everyone the same Google link, no matter what they said.

The legal side has teeth, too. The FTC says its review rule [lets courts impose civil penalties for knowing violations](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-reviews-testimonials-rule-questions-answers). I'm not a lawyer, so run your own process past yours.

## What should a review request text message say?

A review request text should say who you are, thank the customer for something specific to their job, and include one direct link to your Google review page. Keep it short, ask for honest feedback, and never suggest a star rating or words to use.

Here is a template you can copy:

> Hi [First Name], it's [Your Name] from [Your Company]. Thanks for trusting us with your home this week. Would you share your honest experience on Google? It helps your neighbors and helps us improve: [link]. Reply STOP to opt out.

And one follow-up, about three days later, only if no review came in:

> Hi [First Name], [Your Name] again. If you have a minute, we'd still love your honest feedback: [link]. No worries if not. Reply STOP to opt out.

Notice what those texts leave out. There is no "5-star" language. There is no "mention water damage." Plenty of SEO guides tell you to coach keywords, but Google bans requesting specific content. It may not even pay off. Experts in [Whitespark's 2026 ranking factors survey](https://whitespark.ca/local-search-ranking-factors/) placed keywords in reviews at only #36 for the local pack. A [limited Sterling Sky test](https://seroundtable.com/keywords-local-reviews-google-local-rankings-34334.html) found no ranking improvement from them.

Two practical notes on the texting itself:

- **Get permission up front.** Add text consent to your work authorization. Ask your attorney whether a review request counts as marketing under the TCPA.
- **Honor opt-outs fast.** [Day Pitney's summary of the FCC's rules](https://daypitney.com/the-fcc-changes-certain-consent-revocation-rules) describes a 10-business-day window for honoring revocation requests. The FCC also changed parts of these rules in September 2026. Your system should stop texting right away.

## How many Google reviews does a contractor need to rank in the map pack?

No fixed number of Google reviews guarantees a contractor a map pack spot. Google says [more reviews and positive ratings can help your local ranking](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091), but distance and relevance count too. Your real target is the top three companies in your own market.

Here is how the experts in [Whitespark's 2026 survey](https://whitespark.ca/local-search-ranking-factors/) ranked review signals for the local pack:

| Review factor | Rank among local pack factors |
|---|---|
| High numerical Google ratings | #6 |
| Quantity of native Google reviews with text | #9 |
| Recency of reviews | #11 |
| Sustained influx of reviews over time | #14 |
| Keywords in reviews | #36 |
| Owner responses to most reviews | #122 |

Two small case studies from Sterling Sky add useful context. Treat them as clues, not proof. One [saw a ranking boost at 10 reviews](https://seroundtable.com/google-local-reviews-number-34367.html), with less effect after that. In another, a client [stopped asking for reviews and rankings fell](https://www.seroundtable.com/review-recency-impacts-local-rankings-in-google-search-34409.html), then recovered once new reviews came in.

So here is the method I would use:

1. **Search your main terms from your service area.** Try "water damage restoration near me" and your top city.
2. **Write down the top three.** Note their review count, star rating, and how many reviews they got in the last 90 days.
3. **Set a monthly pace.** Match their recent pace, then beat it.
4. **Stay steady.** Do not blast your whole customer list in one week. Whitespark's list favors a sustained flow over bursts.

Now run the math for your own shop. This is my math, using made-up volume. Say you close 15 jobs a month. At [Focus Digital's reported 8% to 14% home services average](https://focus-digital.co/average-review-response-rate/), you would earn one or two reviews a month. At the low end of its SMS range (18%), you would earn about three.

That gap could be worth up to about 18 extra reviews a year from the same jobs. Most restoration companies are not closing hundreds of jobs a month, so every ask counts. And with [74% of consumers looking at the last three months](https://www.brightlocal.com/learn/local-consumer-review-survey/), a steady three a month keeps your profile looking alive.

## How should you respond to negative Google reviews?

You should respond to negative Google reviews quickly, calmly, and with facts, then offer to take the conversation offline. Never threaten the reviewer or offer money to change or remove the review. Your reply is really written for the next homeowner reading it at 2am.

A simple format works:

1. **Thank them and name the issue.** Show you read it.
2. **Own what is yours.** If the crew tracked mud through the house, say so.
3. **Give one next step.** A direct phone number and a person's name.
4. **Keep claim details private.** Do not post insurance details, addresses, or job specifics.

Watch two lines here. Google bans incentives for [revising or removing a negative review](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114). The FTC says you [should not use threats or knowingly false accusations](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-reviews-testimonials-rule-questions-answers) to get one changed or removed.

[Whitespark's survey](https://whitespark.ca/local-search-ranking-factors/) ranks owner responses low for rankings, at #122. Reply anyway. BrightLocal says [42% of consumers are unlikely to use a business that never replies, and 50% are unlikely to choose one with templated replies](https://www.brightlocal.com/learn/local-consumer-review-survey/). So write real replies, not copy-and-paste ones.

## How do you automate Google review requests?

You automate Google review requests by connecting the ask to your job status, so a closed job triggers the same text to every customer. Good automation removes the human memory problem. It should not remove honesty, which means no sentiment filter deciding who sees your Google link.

Before you trust any tool, ask the vendor these questions:

- **Who gets the Google link?** It should be every eligible customer, not only those who rate you highly first.
- **What triggers the send?** It should be job completion, not a monthly batch.
- **Can I edit the wording?** You want to remove any star-rating or keyword coaching.
- **How does it handle STOP replies?** They should end texting automatically.
