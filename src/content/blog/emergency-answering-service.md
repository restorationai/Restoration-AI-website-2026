## What is an emergency answering service?

An emergency answering service is a 24/7 phone service that answers your calls after hours, spots the urgent ones, and escalates them to your on-call staff right away. It follows rules you set: which calls count as emergencies, who gets contacted first, and what happens if that person does not respond.

That is the textbook version. In practice, most services work like this:

1. **A shared operator answers.** One operator often covers many businesses. Your call pops up on their screen with your script.
2. **They read your questions.** Name, address, callback number, and a short note about the problem.
3. **They flag it urgent.** If the caller says "flood" or "sewage," the operator marks it as an emergency.
4. **They escalate.** The operator calls, texts, or patches the call through to your on-call person.
5. **They wait.** If nobody picks up, they try the next number on your list.

Step 5 is where jobs leak out. The homeowner hears "a technician will call you back." Then they keep dialing. In my years on the truck, the jobs we lost almost never went to a better company. They went to whoever called back first.

## Which businesses need an emergency answering service?

Any business where a delay causes damage or danger needs an emergency answering service. That includes water, fire, and mold restoration, plumbing, HVAC, roofing, electrical, locksmiths, and property management. If customers call you at 2am because something is getting worse by the minute, you need coverage that acts at 2am.

Here is a quick way to tell if your trade qualifies:

| Trade | Typical emergency | Why minutes matter |
|---|---|---|
| Water restoration | Burst pipe, sewage backup | Mold can start growing if materials stay wet past [24 to 48 hours](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home) |
| Plumbing | Active leak, no water | Every hour adds damage and cost |
| HVAC | No heat in a freeze, no AC in a heat wave | Health risk for kids and seniors |
| Roofing | Storm damage, open roof | Rain gets inside until a tarp goes on |
| Property management | Tenant flood, lockout, fire | Liability and tenant safety |

Restoration sits at the top of this list for a reason. ## How should an emergency answering service triage calls?

An emergency answering service should triage calls by asking a few trade-specific questions that decide urgency, crew, and equipment before anyone is dispatched. For water damage, that means finding the source, the [IICRC S500](https://iicrc.org/s500/) water category, the affected rooms, ownership, and insurance. A generic "is this an emergency?" question is not triage.

Here is what good triage looks like on a restoration call:

1. **Source:** Supply line, appliance, roof, toilet overflow, or sewer backup?
2. **Category:** Clean water (Cat 1), gray water (Cat 2), or black water (Cat 3)? Sewage changes the PPE, equipment, and urgency.
3. **Scope:** How many rooms? Is water still running? Has it reached a ceiling or lower floor?
4. **Authority:** Does the caller own the property? A tenant usually cannot authorize work.
5. **Insurance:** Who is the carrier, and has a claim been filed?
6. **Safety:** Is there standing water near outlets, a sagging ceiling, or a gas smell?

Most human operators cannot do this well. They are reading a script for dozens of businesses. They rarely know a Cat 1 supply line from a Cat 3 backup.

Good triage also means knowing what is NOT an emergency. Old damage, out-of-area addresses, and solicitors should not wake up your on-call tech. Every false alarm at 3am makes your crew slower to answer the next real one.

> In my six years as a restoration tech, the pattern was always the same: the calls that went sideways were the ones where we rolled with the wrong info. We showed up with air movers for a sewage job, or drove 40 minutes to a tenant who could not sign anything. Triage is not paperwork. It decides whether the first visit makes money.

## How much does an emergency answering service cost?

An emergency answering service typically costs $250 to $1,725 a month on per-minute plans, or $300 to $2,100 a month on per-call plans. That works out to about $3.45 to $5.00 per minute, or $7 to $11.50 per call. Long emergency intake calls, overage, and after-hours volume push the real bill higher.

Here are published 2026 prices from two well-known live services:

| Provider | Plan | Monthly price | Included | Effective rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [Ruby](https://www.ruby.com/pricing/) | Entry | $250 | 50 minutes | $5.00 per minute |
| Ruby | Largest listed | $1,725 | 500 minutes | $3.45 per minute |
| [Smith.ai](https://smith.ai/pricing) | Starter | $300 | 30 calls | $10.00 per call, $11.50 overage |
| Smith.ai | Pro | $2,100 | 300 calls | $7.00 per call, $8.50 overage |

Now apply that to a real emergency call. A thorough water intake takes four to six minutes. At $5.00 a minute, that one call costs $20 to $30 before anyone gets dispatched. That is my math, not a vendor quote. It explains why per-minute services push operators to keep calls short. Short calls mean thin notes, and thin notes mean your tech calls the homeowner back to ask the same questions.

The fee is not the expensive part, though. Run the numbers on what slips through. Say a slow callback costs you two residential water jobs a month. At $3,000 to $7,000 per job, that is $6,000 to $14,000 lost every month. One commercial loss can be $25,000 to $100,000 or more.

Before you sign, ask about:

- **Minute rounding:** Is a 61-second call billed as two minutes?
- **Spam and wrong numbers:** Are they billable?
- **Patch and dispatch fees:** Is escalating to your tech an extra charge?
- **Holiday and storm overage:** What happens to your bill in a freeze?

## What happens to an emergency answering service during a storm?

During a storm, a human emergency answering service is capped by how many operators are on shift, so callers wait on hold, hit overflow voicemail, or get a rushed message. Emergencies come in waves, not one at a time. That is exactly when a staffed call center is stretched thinnest.

Look at the scale. After Winter Storm Uri in 2021, Texas saw about [500,000 insurance claims](https://spectrumlocalnews.com/tx/south-texas-el-paso/news/2022/01/05/february-winter-storm-insurance-claims-at-500k), and roughly 85% were property claims like burst pipes. Every one of those started with a phone call.

The operators answering your line are also answering for every other plumber and restorer they serve in that region. When everyone's phones light up at once, hold times climb. And callers do not wait: an [Invoca consumer survey](https://martechseries.com/analytics/invoca-report-finds-consumers-want-call-businesses-half-satisfied-experience/) found three out of four callers hang up after being placed on hold.

Speed after the answer matters too. A classic [Harvard Business Review study](https://hbr.org/2011/03/the-short-life-of-online-sales-leads) found that firms contacting leads within an hour were nearly seven times more likely to qualify them than firms that waited even one hour longer. I break down more of that research in our post on [speed to lead](/blog/speed-to-lead).

## Can AI handle emergency calls for a contractor?

Yes, AI can handle emergency calls for a contractor, and for most trade emergencies it does more than a human operator can. A well-built AI receptionist answers in seconds, takes unlimited calls at once, asks the trade-specific triage questions every time, and dispatches a tech instead of just paging one.

Here is how the two approaches compare on a restoration emergency:

| | Traditional emergency answering service | AI built for restoration |
|---|---|---|
| Answer speed | Queue-based | Within seconds |
| Storm surge capacity | Limited by operators on shift | Unlimited concurrent calls |
| Cat 1, 2, 3 triage | Only if scripted | Built in (IICRC S500 questions) |
| Bad lead screening | Usually passes everything through | Flags out-of-area, old damage, tenants, and spam |
| Escalation | Pages your on-call list | Cascade dispatch until a tech accepts |
| Work authorization | No | Sent by SMS or email during intake |
| Damage photos | No | Customer uploads photos by text link |
| Job record | Email or manual entry | Pushed to your CRM before the caller hangs up |

Those last few rows are the point. A homeowner who has already signed your work authorization and sent you photos is not calling three more companies. The job is locked in.

To be fair, AI is not perfect for every call. A grieving family after a fire may want a human voice. Good systems handle that with a clean handoff to your team. But for the 2am burst pipe, speed and follow-through win.

## How do you choose an emergency answering service?

Choose an emergency answering service by testing what happens after the call is answered: how it triages, how fast a tech is actually assigned, how it handles volume spikes, and how the job gets into your system. A friendly greeting is table stakes. The outcome of the call is what you are buying.

Use this checklist before you sign anything:

1. **Call it yourself at 2am.** Play a panicked homeowner with water coming through the ceiling. Time how long until a tech is assigned, not just until someone answers.
2. **Ask how it defines an emergency.** Can you set your own rules by trade, water category, and zip code?
3. **Ask how escalation works.** Does it stop after one page, or keep going down your list until someone accepts?
4. **Ask about surge plans.** What happens when 40 calls come in during the same hour?
5. **Ask what the customer gets.** A promise of a callback, or an arrival window, a photo link, and a work authorization?
6. **Ask what you get.** A text with a name, or a recording, transcript, and job record in JobNimbus, Albiware, Jobber, or ServiceTitan?
7. **Ask about billing rules.** Rounding, spam calls, patch fees, and overage.
